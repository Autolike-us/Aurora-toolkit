The one rule behind everything
Consistency beats novelty.

The same face, the same outfit, the same tone, in a new situation every day. People don't fall in love with your best video. They fall in love with recognizing your character in the first half-second of the next one.

Everything here is a method and a record of what worked once, in full view. Nothing promises you views, followers or income.

2 Design

One or two odd features beat a realistic face every time.

A realistic, handsome, generic AI person is invisible: the feed is full of them. A character people stop for has a silhouette a viewer can name from a thumbnail, in half a second, with no caption.

The Toussaint formula

  • One exaggerated feature: a comically long, slender neck.
  • One rigid feature: a tall flat-top fade that never changes shape.
  • One costume anchor: a massive bodybuilder physique in a tight orange 1970s knit cardigan, thick glasses on a chain.
  • One expression: a deadpan, unblinking stare. He never smiles at the joke.
  • One job: a street-level inspector of posture, form and structural integrity. Most things fail.

Design your own the same way

  1. Pick one physical exaggeration a child could draw: a neck, a chin, ears, hair, height, posture.
  2. Pick one outfit worn almost every time. One colour a viewer can spot in a grid.
  3. Pick one expression and hold it. The comedy is the gap between the character's seriousness and the situation.
  4. Give the character one repeatable job or obsession. A job generates infinite episodes; a look alone runs out in a week.
  5. Write it all in one paragraph. That paragraph becomes your identity lock.
The one-second test: show someone a still for one second, then hide it. If they can't describe the character back to you, the design is not finished.

3 The identity lock

Write the lock once. Paste it into every prompt.

AI video tools drift: faces soften, hair shrinks, and your character's key feature quietly disappears by the third generation. The fix is boring and it works: a fixed block of text, the identity lock, pasted into every prompt, word for word.

Toussaint's real lock

COPY MEUncle Toussaint, 49, dignified old-school Haitian uncle: comically long slender neck, massive bodybuilder physique straining inside a tight orange 1970s knit cardigan, tall rigid flat-top fade haircut, thick glasses on a chain, dark weathered skin with visible pores, deadpan unblinking stare. His hands are empty. He takes himself 100% seriously.

What makes a lock work

1 · Name the odd features explicitly.
"His very long neck" and "his tall rigid flat-top fade". The tool protects what you name.
2 · Include behaviour, not just looks.
"He takes himself 100% seriously" is an acting direction, and it is the joke.
3 · Include one restriction.
Toussaint's is "hands empty": no props unless the bit itself is the prop.
4 · Freeze it.
Improve the wording? Change it everywhere at once and note the date. Two locks means two characters.

Build your own: the template

TEMPLATE[Name], [age], [one-line persona]: [exaggerated feature], [second rigid feature], [signature outfit], [hair / head feature], [skin / surface detail], [default expression]. [One restriction, e.g. hands empty.] [One behaviour line: how seriously they take themselves.]
When the face driftsAdd more reference angles and shorten the source clip. Drift compounds.

4 The reference pack

Five angles at one scale hold a face better than fifty random stills.

Text alone will not hold a character. Video tools that accept reference images hold the face when the references agree with each other. Build the pack once, before your first real video.

Build yours in this order

  1. The sheet first: five full-body views, same scale, same outfit, same light.
  2. Three close-ups: front, three-quarter and side profile, chest-up, in the default expression.
  3. Two backup singles: a front chest-up and a pure side profile. The profile is what proves the exaggerated feature.
  4. One alternate outfit only if the character truly needs it, kept in a separate folder so it never leaks into the default look.

Do it free with Gemini and ChatGPT

STEP A · ChatGPT

Turn your idea into a lock.
Tell it your one exaggeration, outfit, expression and job, and ask it to write a single tight paragraph with a behaviour line and one restriction. Save the result as your lock.

STEP B · Gemini

Turn the lock into the sheet.
Paste the lock and ask for a character sheet: five full-body angles at one scale, plain grey background, same light, then close-ups. Regenerate until the odd feature is identical in every view.

SAMPLE PROMPTCharacter reference sheet. [PASTE YOUR IDENTITY LOCK]. Five full-body views at the same scale (front, three-quarter front, side, three-quarter back, back). Plain light-grey studio background, soft even light, same outfit in every view. Below: three chest-up close-ups (front, three-quarter, side profile).

When a video tool lets you add more references, add angles, not new outfits.

5 Prompts

Camera, Tone, Lighting, Texture. In that order, every time.

A prompt written as a pile of adjectives gives you a pile of results. The structure that repeats is four parts, one or two sentences each, always in the same order, with the identity lock inside the Tone part.

PartWhat it decides
1 · CameraLens and framing: "35mm lens, slightly low side view, full-body framing, slight motion blur." This is what makes it feel shot, not rendered.
2 · ToneMood first, then who is in it, doing what, where. The identity lock lives here.
3 · LightingDirection and quality: "Harsh midday sun, hard shadows on the pavement." One light source, named.
4 · TextureMaterials and surface detail: knit, skin pores, cracked concrete, film grain. Texture is where realism actually comes from.

A complete, real example: the sidewalk inspection

REAL PROMPTCamera: Candid 35mm film photograph, slightly low side view, full-body framing, crooked composition, slight motion blur.
Tone: Deadpan, serious and absurd, like a formal inspection caught by a passerby. [IDENTITY LOCK] He stands rigidly inspecting a cracked sidewalk, deadpan and unblinking, hands empty.
Lighting: Harsh midday sun, hard shadows on the pavement, bright foreground and darker street behind.
Texture: Tight knit cardigan texture, dark weathered skin with visible pores, rough cracked concrete, dust and film grain.
For video
Keep the same four parts and add one action with a clear start and end. One beat per clip (chapter 7).
Pro tip
Ask ChatGPT to fill the four parts for a new situation, then paste your lock in yourself so it never gets reworded.

6 Formats

Steal the format, not the video.

New accounts don't have time to invent formats and test them. The platforms already tell you what's working: it's the video with two million views in your niche this week. Take that format (the structure, the timing, the beat) and put your character in it.

The rules

  • Remake the structure, never repost the video. Their clip is research, not content.
  • One clear person, clean light, similar build in the source clip makes the cleanest swap.
  • Your character must change the joke. If the video would work identically with anyone in it, your character is decoration, not the star.
  • Keep a shortlist. Save candidate formats as you scroll, review them weekly, and shoot the top two or three, not all of them.

Formats that fit an inspector character

🎯 The deadpan test that fails
Archery at a disc, a hurdle, a deadlift: attempt, fail, verdict.
🤫 The silent professional
He does an ordinary job (chef, barista) without a word, then walks off.
👯 The twin test
He inspects an identical copy of himself, and fails him.
🏗️ The public inspection
A mundane place or object, graded like a building site.
⏱ Spacing rule: do not run the same beat twice in a row. Two fall endings back to back turn a surprise into a routine. Alternate: a fall, then a silent one, then an inspection.

7 Production

Short beats, stitched, at a cost you know before you start.

AI video is sold in seconds, so produce in seconds. Plan each generation as one beat of about 8 to 16 seconds with a clear start and end, then stitch beats together in an editor such as CapCut. A 30-second video is three good beats, not one long drifting generation.

The workflow

  1. Hook in the first second. Open on the verdict moment (the crack, the lean, the fail), not on a walk-in.
  2. Generate the beat in Higgsfield with your character sheet as the reference and your identity lock in the prompt.
  3. Check the lock before anything else: face, key feature, outfit, expression. Drifted? Regenerate that beat only.
  4. Keep audio ambient in the generation (street noise, room tone). Add the trending sound later, on the platform, so it stays current.
  5. Stitch, caption, post. The caption is written before you generate, not after (chapter 8).

8 Captions

A caption is a verdict: under 15 words, zero hashtags.

The video is the evidence. The caption is the ruling. Toussaint's captions follow a formal-report formula: what was inspected, what failed, what it means. Short sentences, completely deadpan, never winking at the joke.

Real captions from the account

The formula, for any character

  • One short verdict, under 15 words.
  • Zero hashtags. The character is the discovery mechanism, not the tags.
  • Emojis almost never. For Toussaint, only the flag or the ruler, occasionally.
  • Never explain in the caption that the character is AI. Labelling AI content is a platform setting (next chapter), not copy.
  • Write the caption in the character's voice, not yours. If the character wouldn't say it in a formal report, rewrite it.

9 Posting

Every platform, same character, labelled as AI.

A character compounds when the same person appears everywhere a viewer might land. Post the same video to Instagram, TikTok and YouTube Shorts and let each platform find its own audience for it.

  • Post everywhere, at the same time. Don't hold a platform back to "test". The test is the posting.
  • Turn on the AI label on every post, in the platform's own setting. It protects the account and it's increasingly the honest norm. The joke survives it, because the character never pretended to be a documentary.
  • Link in bio from day one. Even before you have a product, it's where future money lands: a tip jar, a storefront, or a sales page.
  • Consistency over perfection. A good-enough video today beats a perfect one next week. The lock makes "good enough" still look like the same person.
  • Collaborations count double. Post collabs as a true platform collaboration (both profiles), not a tag in the caption.
The silhouette test
If the side profile doesn't read instantly in the thumbnail, the post isn't ready. Check it before you publish.

10 Your data

Viewers are not followers, and their map lives in the reel, not the profile.

The most common data mistake with a viral AI character: opening follower demographics and calling it your audience. When about 98% of views come from people who don't follow you, follower data describes almost nobody who watched. Viewer data is per-video.

Check your viewers yourself (Instagram)

  1. Open the reel and tap View insights under the video.
  2. Read accounts reached / views, watch time, shares, and follows from this reel. Shares and follows-per-view are the numbers that predict the next hit.
  3. For where viewers are, use the audience section of that reel's insights where your account and region offer it, and the Professional Dashboard's audience view for the account overall. Treat follower geography only as a partial map, clearly labelled as followers, not viewers.
  4. In the app, Settings and activity → Insights gives account-level reach and the content that drove it over the last 30 to 90 days. (TikTok has the same idea: open a video and tap More insights.)

Earn in the order it unlocks. Don't wait for the platform to pay you.

At a few thousand followers, Instagram is proof, not a payer. The money that's actually available early comes from people and brands paying for what the character can do. Here is the ladder, in the order it realistically opens.

12 Sell the system

Your process is a product, and the market has already priced it.

Once your character works, the method itself becomes sellable, because thousands of people want the result you already have. This isn't theory: it's what creators in this exact niche are doing. When this playbook was researched, comparable AI-character playbooks and courses were selling at roughly $14.99, $19.99 and $29.90 to $69.90, usually as a PDF plus extras.

How to package yours

  1. Lead with your case study. Your dated, real numbers are the sales page. Screenshots beat adjectives.
  2. Price inside the proven band. Around $19.99 to $29 for a PDF playbook; move higher only if you add a video walkthrough or a tool. A short launch price (this playbook launched at $9 for two weeks, then $29) gives early buyers a reason to act.
  3. Sell where creators already buy: a store link in your bio and on your showcase site.
  4. Give the templates away inside the product, not on the sales page: the lock, the prompt structure, the caption bank, the checklists.
  5. Promise the method, never the outcome. Say what the system is and what it did on your account, with dates. That keeps you honest and out of refund trouble: results depend on the buyer's character and execution.
🔁 The loop

The character promotes the playbook, the playbook teaches new characters, and every buyer's result becomes social proof for both. Your account is the ad. This PDF is the product.

15 Honest limits

What breaks a character, and what nobody can promise you.

Lock breaks to catch before you post

👕 Costume drift
A random outfit (a fringed jacket instead of the cardigan) makes the character a stranger for one post. Role uniforms, like a chef coat for a kitchen bit, are the exception because the job explains them.
🌀 AI morphs
A body or face that changes mid-clip pulls every eye to the glitch. If it wasn't intentional, regenerate: viewers will comment on the morph, not the joke.
🖐 Ambiguous gestures
A glitched hand can read as a rude gesture to camera. Scrub the last seconds of every clip before posting.
🎭 Tone breaks
A bit that makes the character creepy instead of strict (peeking instead of inspecting) damages the persona the account is built on. Hold those clips.
🏷 Branding accidents. A visible product logo in a gag reads as an unpaid ad, and a powder-on-the-nose joke reads as a drug gag. Decide both deliberately, never by accident.

The honest limits of this playbook

One account, one viral post, under two weeks. That is a real result and a small sample. The method raises your odds (a recognizable character, proven formats, consistent posting, honest data) but no playbook can promise a viral post, a follower count or an income, and you shouldn't buy or sell one that does.

What this system does give you is cheaper learning: every video costs about the price of a coffee, every mistake is visible in the lock-check, and every result is in your own insights within a day.
